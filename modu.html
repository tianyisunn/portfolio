01
Overview

The idea

Build the furniture.
Build your own space.

MODU is a gamified mobile experience that teaches furniture assembly through interactive 3D guidance. It adapts how much support users receive, then turns each completed build into furniture for a personal virtual room.

How might we lower the cognitive load of furniture assembly while preserving independence, choice and a sense of achievement?

The challenge

Flat-pack instructions assume one kind of learner.

Assembly can require planning, working memory, attention, spatial reasoning and recovery from mistakes—all at once.

The response

One clear action at a time.

Users can see the next part, rotate a 3D model, ask for hints and choose a guidance style without losing control of the task.

02
Why MODU

Problem framing

Guidance is usually fixed.
People are not.

The problem

Dense manuals place the recovery work on the user.

A missed step or misread diagram can create uncertainty that compounds across the entire build.

The solution

Adaptive support without taking over.

  • Show one action, part and tool at a time
  • Offer four support profiles after a short questionnaire
  • Use hints, undo and retry loops to make mistakes safe
Why gamification?

Visible progress, rewards and room customisation give users a reason to return rather than abandon an unfinished build.

Why IKEA?

A recognisable, standardised parts system gave us a real instruction set to design and test against.

MODU
Why the name MODU?

MODU comes from “modular.”

I proposed the name to connect two parts of the product: flat-pack furniture is built from repeatable modules, while MODU's guidance can also be reconfigured around each user's support needs.

01ModularityA flexible system rather than one fixed instruction flow.
02Modular furnitureRepeatable parts and fasteners create a realistic learning context.
03Modular supportUsers can change the pace, format and amount of help they receive.

Brand system: MODU is the central in-app guide, while four avatars represent different support styles without turning them into clinical labels.

04
Research

Research synthesis

From six diagnosis-led profiles to
four task-based needs.

We reviewed approximately 20 papers and sources covering ADHD, autism, AuDHD, dyslexia and dyspraxia. The recurring assembly difficulties crossed diagnostic categories and could also affect first-time assemblers. We treated these patterns as design hypotheses, not clinical profiles.

Starting point Six diagnosis-led profiles

Early concepts connected guidance styles to named cognitive conditions.

What changed Needs overlapped

Planning, information processing, spatial reasoning and reassurance appeared across categories.

Product decision Four adjustable support needs

Guidance would respond to the task and the user's preference, without asking for a diagnosis.

01

Executive function

Planning, starting, sequencing and returning to a task.

02

Information processing

Controlling the amount, pace and form of information.

03

Spatial reasoning

Understanding orientation, parts and movement in three dimensions.

04

Cognitive safety

Predictability, reassurance and low-risk recovery from mistakes.

What changed in the product: onboarding questions moved toward everyday preferences, recommended modes became guidance preferences rather than identities, and support remained adjustable during the build.

Design principle: support is always available, but users control how much they receive and how it is presented.

Testing

Formative testing

Five participants tested
the end-to-end prototype.

Participants used the mobile or iPad experience and completed a post-task survey. All had assembled flat-pack furniture a few times and had slight to moderate familiarity with interactive 3D instructions. The sample included mixed self-described cognitive profiles, so we looked for usability signals rather than subgroup differences.

5/5selected step-by-step task segmentation as helpful
4.8/5average rating for completion feedback clarity
4.6/5average rating for visual instruction clarity
3.2/5average rating for ease of 3D model controls
Keep
Small steps and clear completion feedback were the strongest parts.

All five participants selected step-by-step segmentation as helpful. Visual instructions and completion feedback were among the highest-rated assembly elements.

Simplify
3D interaction remained the clearest usability risk.

Ease of 3D controls averaged 3.2/5, and three participants selected 3D controls as an area for improvement. The next iteration needs larger targets, forgiving snapping and faster actions.

Orient
Participants did not always know where they were in the build.

Stage awareness averaged 3.4/5 and ranged from 1 to 5. An overview, visible target model and stronger current-step cues would make the journey easier to scan.

Clarify
Onboarding and personalised guidance worked unevenly.

Initial questions averaged 4.8/5, but three participants still selected onboarding for improvement and one rated the recommended guidance 2/5. Profile switching needs clearer explanation and an optional guided transition.

Compare
MODU was not universally preferred to a paper manual.

Perceived helpfulness versus a paper manual averaged 3.6/5 and ranged from 2 to 5. One participant wanted a fast overview video rather than waiting through every 3D interaction.

Motivation signal: all five participants agreed that rewards made completion more meaningful, and all selected new furniture tasks as a reason they might return. This reflects stated preference, not measured retention.

Research limitation: this was a five-person convenience sample using a self-report survey. We did not record task time, error rate or a controlled comparison with paper instructions. The findings set iteration priorities; they do not validate clinical fit, subgroup differences or real-world assembly performance.

Reflection

What I learned

Accessibility works best as
product logic, not an add-on.

The most important shift was moving away from designing “for a diagnosis” and toward designing flexible support around a task. That changed the onboarding, data model, guidance patterns and testing questions—not just the visual interface.

Next

Validate the support model

Test whether profile recommendations match what users actually prefer during a real build.

Next

Reduce input friction

Refine touch controls, snapping and part highlighting across phone and tablet devices.

Next

Measure the right outcomes

Compare task comprehension, confidence, recovery and perceived cognitive load—not only completion.
