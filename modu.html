Overview

The idea

Build the furniture.
Build your own space.

MODU is a gamified mobile experience that teaches furniture assembly through interactive 3D guidance. It adapts how much support users receive, then turns each completed build into furniture for a personal virtual room.

How might we lower the cognitive load of furniture assembly while preserving independence, choice and a sense of achievement?

The challenge

Flat-pack instructions assume one kind of learner.

Assembly can require planning, working memory, attention, spatial reasoning and recovery from mistakes—all at once.

The response

One clear action at a time.

Users can see the next part, rotate a 3D model, ask for hints and choose a guidance style without losing control of the task.

02
Why MODU

Problem framing

Guidance is usually fixed.
People are not.

The problem

Dense manuals place the recovery work on the user.

A missed step or misread diagram can create uncertainty that compounds across the entire build.

The solution

Adaptive support without taking over.

  • Show one action, part and tool at a time
  • Offer four support profiles after a short questionnaire
  • Use hints, undo and retry loops to make mistakes safe
Why gamification?

Visible progress, rewards and room customisation give users a reason to return rather than abandon an unfinished build.

Why IKEA?

A recognisable, standardised parts system gave us a real instruction set to design and test against.

Users & Gap

Audience

Two audiences, one shared need:
progress that feels possible.

01

Support-seeking assemblers

  • Limited assembly experience or confidence
  • Attention, processing or spatial challenges
  • A need for predictable, adjustable guidance
  • A desire to practise before the real build
02

Cozy game players

  • Enjoy relaxing, creative and rewarding play
  • Earn XP and coins by building furniture
  • Personalise a virtual room
  • Visit friends and share spaces

Existing tools support visualisation, instruction or engagement—but rarely personalised support during real furniture assembly.

04
Research

Research synthesis

From diagnostic labels to
support needs.

Our early concepts grouped users by diagnosis. Research showed that this was too rigid: people can share a diagnosis and still need very different support. We reframed the model around what a user needs during a task.

01

Executive function

Planning, starting, sequencing and returning to a task.

02

Information processing

Controlling the amount, pace and form of information.

03

Spatial reasoning

Understanding orientation, parts and movement in three dimensions.

04

Cognitive safety

Predictability, reassurance and low-risk recovery from mistakes.

Design principle: support is always available, but users control how much they receive and how it is presented.

Experience architecture

A complete loop from
first question to finished room.

The product combines an adaptive setup flow, an interactive assembly session and a cosy reward loop. The same profile informs guidance without locking the user into a permanent mode.

Testing

Formative testing

Testing surfaced friction
before it became polish.

Participants tested the prototype on mobile and iPad. We observed how they moved through the tutorial, interpreted highlighting and controlled the 3D assembly experience.

Readability
Important UI elements felt too small.

Increase touch targets and treat tablet layouts as intentional breakpoints rather than scaled mobile screens.

Guidance
The target part was not always obvious.

Strengthen colour contrast and combine highlighting with clearer spatial cues.

Controls
Screw placement and joystick movement felt imprecise.

Tune sensitivity, enlarge interactive zones and provide more forgiving snapping behaviour.

Onboarding
The tutorial felt long and easy to skip.

Shorten the initial sequence and teach controls closer to the moment they are needed.

Research limitation: the sample was formative and too small to validate support profiles or make claims about clinical effectiveness. The findings guide iteration; they do not prove outcomes.

Reflection

What I learned

Accessibility works best as
product logic, not an add-on.

The most important shift was moving away from designing “for a diagnosis” and toward designing flexible support around a task. That changed the onboarding, data model, guidance patterns and testing questions—not just the visual interface.

Next

Validate the support model

Test whether profile recommendations match what users actually prefer during a real build.

Next

Reduce input friction

Refine touch controls, snapping and part highlighting across phone and tablet devices.

Next

Measure the right outcomes

Compare task comprehension, confidence, recovery and perceived cognitive load—not only completion.
