01
The Problem

There's an engagement gap at the heart of Irish literary heritage

James Joyce is a cornerstone of Dublin's identity — yet standing before a plaque at 7 Eccles Street, most visitors feel nothing. Traditional museum and street exhibits rely on static text and bronze markers that speak to historians, not to the curious traveler pulling out their phone.

Our design brief: How might we transform passive monument viewing into active literary interaction — for the modern traveler, the bookworm, and the history lover?

"How do we make a 110-year-old short story feel urgent on a busy Dublin street?"

02

Through field investigations at the Dublin Writers Museum and in-depth user interviews, we found the problem wasn't Joyce's prose being too complex — it was the constant attention-switching between a physical sign and the reader's imagination.

The friction isn't literary. It's spatial. People can't hold a mental image of Eveline's parlour while simultaneously reading about it on a rain-streaked plaque.

We interviewed three participants representing our core personas: the Traveler (cultural curiosity, limited literary background), the Bookworm (deep textual knowledge, wants contextual depth), and the History Lover (place-driven, archival mindset). Each revealed a different failure mode of the current experience.

03
The Idea

A living narrative overlay — the city as the gallery

We proposed Retellings — a mobile-first web experience that uses location-aware triggers and minimalist visual metaphors to layer Joyce's Dubliners directly onto the streets that inspired it.

The design principle: bring the story to the user's feet, not make them travel mentally to reach it. Each landmark becomes a chapter entrance. The UI disappears into the city rather than competing with it.

04
Testing

Our first instinct was wrong — and the users told us immediately

Our initial low-fidelity prototypes leaned heavily on Augmented Reality visual overlays. In walkthroughs on Grafton Street and along the Liffey, users quickly flagged two critical problems:

01
Visual Competition

Heavy AR overlays blocked the physical beauty of Dublin itself — the very thing users came to experience.

02
Safety Concerns

Navigating busy city streets while looking through an AR lens created real anxiety. Users chose phone safety over engagement.

03
Cognitive Load — Again

We'd solved one context-switching problem only to create another. The AR had become its own kind of plaque.

This was a crucial pivot point. User feedback directly drove our next iteration rather than technical ambition.

05
Iteration

Shifting to Audio-First — less is more in environmental UX

We made a bold call: deprioritize visuals entirely. The new model was "Audio-First, Visual-Second" — let your ears carry Joyce while your eyes carry you safely through the city.

As lead front-end developer, I refactored the entire interface around this principle. The visual layer became a calm, typographic companion — ambient rather than immersive. Clean accessible markup, performant across all mobile devices.

30% increase in user dwell time after the pivot. Removing friction, not adding features, drove the improvement.
